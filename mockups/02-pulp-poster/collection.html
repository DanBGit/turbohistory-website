Why this order

Every one of these six read the last one. Caesar carried Alexander around in his head and wept that he had done nothing by the age Alexander had done everything. Napoleon studied Caesar and Hannibal on campaign and wrote about them in exile. Conquest is a copied trick, passed down the centuries by men who each believed they were the original.

So the collection runs chronologically, and each book is picked for what it adds to the argument rather than for how famous the name is. Alexander sets the template. Hannibal shows what happens when brilliance meets a state that simply refuses to lose. Caesar turns an army into a government. William proves one afternoon can rewrite a country. Genghis builds the largest land empire ever assembled and then hands it to sons who spend it. Napoleon does the whole cycle again in twenty years, on camera, with paperwork.

The reading path

Six stops, in order · roughly one hour each

1Start here

Alexander the Great: The Boy Who Conquered the World by Thirty-Two

356–323 BC · Macedon to the Indus

Start here because everyone after him started here too. Alexander invents the job: the young commander who fights at the front, keeps moving, and treats the known world as a to-do list. He dies at thirty-two with no plan for the morning after, and his generals carve up the empire within a decade. The template and its fatal flaw arrive in the same book.

Then go to Hannibal, who inherits the template and runs it against Rome

2

Hannibal: Elephants Over the Alps

247–183 BC · Carthage against Rome

The best tactician in the collection and the one who loses. Hannibal takes an army over the Alps, destroys Roman field armies three times in three years, and still cannot make Rome admit it is beaten. Read second because it is the counter-argument to Alexander: winning every battle is not the same as winning, and a stubborn state beats a brilliant man.

Then go to Julius Caesar, who is the state learning to fight back and then eating itself

Read the Book Page
3

Julius Caesar: The Dictator Stabbed by His Best Friend

100–44 BC · Gaul, the Rubicon, the Senate floor

Caesar is the hinge of the collection. He conquers Gaul, turns the loyalty of an army into political power at home, and breaks the republic that made him. Read third for the shift from conquest as adventure to conquest as government — and for the lesson every later name on this list ignored, delivered by twenty-three knives.

Then go to William, who compresses the whole thing into a single day

Read the Book Page
4

William the Conqueror: How a Bastard Duke Took England in One Day

1028–1087 · Normandy and England

The shortest conquest and the most complete. One battle on one afternoon in October 1066 replaces an entire ruling class, the language of power, and the shape of the country's law. Read fourth to see the machinery close up: William survives by counting what he took, which is why the Domesday Book exists and why his conquest outlasted him.

Then go to Genghis Khan, who does the same thing at eighty times the scale

Read the Book Page
5

Genghis Khan: From Outcast Boy to Emperor of the World

1162–1227 · Mongolia to the Caspian

The largest contiguous land empire in history, built by a man who began as an outcast boy on the steppe, hunting marmots to survive. Read fifth because the scale finally breaks the pattern you have been watching: Genghis builds an administration, a postal relay, a written law, and a succession plan. It still splinters within two generations.

Then finish with Napoleon, who runs the entire arc again in twenty years

6Finish here

Napoleon: The Corsican Outsider Who Took France

1769–1821 · Corsica, Paris, Moscow, Saint Helena

Finish here because Napoleon had read all five of the others and thought he had solved them. He gets everything Alexander wanted and everything Caesar built, adds a legal code that still runs in six countries, and loses it all inside twenty years — ending on a rock in the Atlantic with roughly six million dead behind him and the systems he built still running without him.

End of the path. The whole argument is in the last twenty pages of this one

Finished all six?

The pattern you have just watched six times over does not stop in 1821. If you want the modern version, read World War I and then World War II back to back — the same argument about power, run by states instead of by men, with the casualty figures multiplied.
