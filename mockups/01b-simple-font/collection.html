Conquest is the one subject school gets backwards. You get the battles and the dates, and never the thing that actually explains them: every conqueror on this list was copying somebody. Alexander wrote the manual. Hannibal used it against Rome. Rome learned from Hannibal and handed the lesson to Caesar, who used it on Rome itself. Genghis Khan built the same machine from outside the classical world entirely and made it bigger than all of them. William proved you could do it in a single afternoon if you picked the right afternoon. Napoleon tried the whole thing one last time with modern armies, and found the ceiling.

So the six books are ordered as an argument rather than a timeline, though it lands close to chronological anyway. Read straight through and you watch one idea — move faster than anyone can react, then govern what you took — get picked up, sharpened, industrialised, and finally broken. Each book stands alone and starts from nothing, so you can jump in anywhere. The order is where the collection pays.

Six books, an hour each, roughly five hours end to end. That is a long train journey, or a week of commutes, for two thousand years of empire. Reading times are honest estimates from word count at roughly 250 words per minute.

The reading path

Six books, in the order that makes each one hit harder than it would alone.

01

Alexander the Great — The Boy Who Conquered the World by Thirty-Two

356–323 BC · Macedon to the Indus · Under an hour

Start here because everyone after him is working from this template. Eleven years, no defeat, an empire from Greece to the Punjab, dead at thirty-two with no plan for what came next. It gives you the whole pattern in its purest form: speed as a weapon, a father’s army inherited and improved, and the part nobody warns you about, which is that conquering is the easy half.

02

Hannibal — Elephants Over the Alps

247–183 BC · Carthage against Rome · Under an hour

Second, because Hannibal is Alexander’s method aimed at a state that refuses to lose. Cannae is still taught in war colleges and it still did not win him the war. Read after Alexander and the contrast does the work for you: the same genius, the same speed, a very different enemy, and fifteen years stranded in Italy waiting for a surrender that never came.

Read the book›

03

Julius Caesar — The Dictator Stabbed by His Best Friend

100–44 BC · Gaul, then Rome itself · Under an hour

Third, because this is the state that beat Hannibal turning the weapon on itself. Caesar conquers Gaul, writes his own press coverage while doing it, then marches the army home. He is the first man on this list to prove that the real prize was never foreign territory, and the first to find out what a republic does to someone who works that out loud.

Read the book›

04

Genghis Khan — From Outcast Boy to Emperor of the World

c.1162–1227 · The largest land empire ever assembled · Under an hour

Fourth, because the Mediterranean is not the world, and this is the moment the collection admits it. An outcast child eating roots on the steppe builds an empire four times the size of Alexander’s, with a postal service, a written law and religious tolerance bolted on, and a body count that still shows up in population estimates. Everything the first three did on a peninsula, done on a continent.

Read the book›

05

William the Conqueror — How a Bastard Duke Took England in One Day

c.1028–1087 · Normandy to England · Under an hour

Fifth, for scale. This is the smallest conquest on the list and the one you are still living inside. One battle, one afternoon, and a country’s language, landholding, aristocracy and law rebuilt from the ground up, then audited in the Domesday Book. Put next to Genghis it looks trivial. Nine centuries later it is the one that never came undone.

Read the book›

06

Napoleon — The Corsican Outsider Who Took Europe

1769–1821 · Corsica to Saint Helena · Under an hour

Finish here, because Napoleon is the last man to try all of it at once and the only one whose paperwork outlived his empire. He read Alexander and Caesar and said so. He moved faster than anyone in Europe could react, exactly as they had. Then the ceiling: Russia, six million dead, an island in the South Atlantic. What survived was the boring half he built between campaigns — the Code, the prefects, the bank, the baccalaureate, the metric system, still running under the law of six countries.
